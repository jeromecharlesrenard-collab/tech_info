Overview

Subtractive vs. Formative vs. Additive

TopicsRemove · Shape · Add · How many parts
Subtractive
Removes material from a solid block: milling, turning, drilling, grinding, EDM, cutting. Tight tolerances and full material strength, but more waste and limits on internal shapes.
Formative
Shapes material without removing it: molding, casting, forging, sheet metal forming. Cheapest per part at volume, but molds and dies cost time and money.
Additive (AM)
Builds the part layer by layer from a 3D model (3D printing). Little waste, no tooling, complex shapes (lattices, internal channels); slower per part and usually weaker between layers.
CNC
Computer Numerical Control: the machine follows a G-code program. CNC mills and lathes (subtractive) and 3D printers (additive) are all numerically controlled.
How many parts
1 – 10: 3D print or CNC; 10 – 50: vacuum casting; thousands: injection molding, die casting, or stamping.
ItemRemember
SubtractiveCuts away from a block (machining)
FormativeMolds, casts, bends
AdditiveAdds layers (3D printing)
QuantityFew = print or machine; dozens = cast; thousands = mold

Subtractive (Material Removal)

Machining & Cutting Processes

MethodsMachining · Grinding · EDM · Laser · Waterjet · Plasma
Milling
A rotating multi-point cutter removes material from a stationary or moving workpiece: flat surfaces, pockets, slots, contours, and complex 3D shapes. Runs on 3-axis through 5-axis machines.
Turning (lathing)
The workpiece rotates while a stationary single-point tool removes material: shafts, bushings, threads, and tapers. Swiss-type lathes handle small, high-precision parts.
CNC machining
Computer-controlled milling, turning, routing, drilling, and grinding, driven by G-code from CAM software; repeatable, tight-tolerance parts from prototypes to mid volumes.
Drilling / boring / reaming
Making holes, enlarging them, and finishing them to a precise diameter and surface finish.
Grinding
An abrasive wheel removes small amounts of material for very tight tolerances and fine finishes. Types: surface, cylindrical, centerless, jig.
EDM
Electrical Discharge Machining: controlled sparks erode conductive material. Wire EDM cuts profiles; sinker EDM forms cavities. Suited to hardened steels and mold tooling.
Laser cutting
A focused laser beam melts or vaporizes sheet or plate: fast, precise, narrow kerf; metals, plastics, and wood.
Waterjet cutting
A high-pressure water stream, often with abrasive, cuts almost any material with no heat-affected zone.
Plasma cutting
An ionized gas arc cuts conductive metals; faster and cheaper than laser on thick plate, but less precise.
ItemRemember
MillingThe tool spins (flat faces, pockets)
TurningThe part spins (round parts)
GrindingTightest tolerances, finest finish
EDMSparks: hardened steel, molds
CuttingLaser = precise; plasma = thick and cheap; waterjet = no heat

Why Machining Is Much Faster Today

FactorsTool materials · Spindles · Toolpaths · Machines
Tool materials
Coated carbide, ceramics, and CBN (cubic boron nitride) cut steel several times faster than older high-speed steel (HSS) tools. In aluminum, cutting speeds can now pass 1,000 m/min (~3,300 ft/min).
Spindles
High-speed machining centers run at 20,000 to 40,000 rpm or more (standard ones ~8,000 – 15,000); older machines topped out around 6,000 to 10,000 rpm.
Toolpaths
Adaptive (high-efficiency) and trochoidal strategies keep the load on the tool steady, using the full flute length at a light side step, so the tool runs much faster without breaking.
Machine and control
Stiffer frames, faster drives, and controls that read the toolpath ahead (look-ahead) let the machine actually reach those speeds and hold accuracy.

Spindles20,000+ rpm vs. ~8,000 before

MachinesStiffer frames, look-ahead controls

Formative (Shaping Without Removal)

Molding, Casting & Metal Forming

MethodsMolding · Extrusion · Casting · Forging · Sheet metal
Injection molding
Molten thermoplastic is injected under pressure into a closed steel or aluminum mold. High tooling cost, but very low unit cost at high volume, with excellent repeatability.
Extrusion
Material is pushed through a die to make continuous constant-section profiles: tubes, rods, channels, frames. Aluminum, plastics, rubber.
Blow molding
An air-inflated parison (hollow tube of hot plastic) is shaped against a mold: hollow parts like bottles and tanks. Variants: extrusion, injection, and stretch blow molding.
Rotational molding
Plastic powder in a rotating heated mold coats the walls: large, seamless hollow parts with low tooling cost.
Thermoforming / vacuum forming
Heated plastic sheet is drawn over a mold by vacuum or pressure: trays, enclosures, packaging.
Compression / transfer molding
Material is pressed into a heated mold cavity; common for thermosets, rubber, and composites.
Casting
Molten metal is poured or injected into a mold.
  • Sand casting: cheap molds, large parts
  • Die casting: high volume, aluminum and zinc
  • Investment casting (lost wax): fine detail
  • Permanent mold casting: reusable metal molds
Forging
Metal is shaped by compressive force, hot or cold, which aligns the grain for superior strength. Open-die, closed-die, and roll forging.
Stamping / press forming
Sheet metal is cut and formed in dies (blanking, punching, bending, drawing, coining); progressive dies allow very high volume.
Deep drawing
Sheet is pulled into a die cavity: seamless cups, cans, housings.
Sheet metal bending
Press brakes bend sheet along straight lines: brackets, chassis, enclosures.
Roll forming
Strip passes through successive roller stations: long, uniform profiles.
Metal spinning
A rotating disc is formed over a mandrel: axisymmetric parts like cones and domes.
Powder metallurgy / MIM
Metal powder is pressed, or injection molded with a binder (MIM, Metal Injection Molding), then sintered: near-net-shape small, complex metal parts.
ItemRemember
Injection moldingCostly mold, pennies per part at volume
Hollow plasticBlow molding (bottles); roto molding (big tanks)
ExtrusionPush through a die: same profile all along
CastingPour metal: sand, die, investment (lost wax)
ForgingSqueeze metal: strongest parts
Sheet metalStamp, draw, bend, roll, spin

Vacuum Casting

TopicsMaster · Silicone mold · PU resin · Short runs
How it works
A master part (often an SLA print) is cast into a silicone mold; liquid polyurethane (PU) resin is then poured into the mold under vacuum, so there are no air bubbles.
Run size
About 15 – 25 parts per mold; ideal for 10 – 50 parts.
Strengths
Production-like finish and colors; resins that mimic ABS, PP, PC, or rubber (wide Shore hardness range); no costly steel mold.
Limits
Molds wear out; slower per part than injection molding; parts are PU, not the real thermoplastic.
ItemRemember
MoldSilicone, made from a printed master
MaterialPU resin poured under vacuum (no bubbles)
Run~20 parts per mold

Additive (3D Printing)

3D Printing Processes

ProcessesFDM · SLA / DLP · SLS / MJF · DMLS / SLM · Binder jet · DED
FDM / FFF
Fused Deposition Modeling (Fused Filament Fabrication): a nozzle melts plastic filament and lays it down line by line. Cheapest and most common (desktop printers); PLA, PETG, ABS, ASA, TPU, nylon, PC. Visible layer lines; weakest across layers (Z direction).
SLA / DLP
Stereolithography: a UV laser (or a projector or screen in DLP and MSLA printers) cures liquid photopolymer resin. Finest detail and smoothest surface (miniatures, dental, jewelry molds); parts need washing (IPA) and UV post-curing; standard resins are brittle.
SLS
Selective Laser Sintering: a laser fuses nylon powder (PA12, PA11). The loose powder supports the part, so no support structures; strong functional parts with a slightly grainy surface.
MJF
Multi Jet Fusion (HP): inkjet heads print a fusing agent on nylon powder, then infrared heat fuses each layer. Like SLS but faster, with more even strength; parts come out gray, usually dyed black.
DMLS / SLM
Metal 3D printing (Direct Metal Laser Sintering / Selective Laser Melting): a laser fuses metal powder into dense, complex metal parts for aerospace, medical, and tooling.
Binder jetting
A binder bonds powder layers, then the part is sintered: faster and cheaper for metal or sand (casting molds) at volume.
DED
Directed Energy Deposition: wire or powder is melted as it is deposited; large parts and repair.

Joining & Assembly

Joining Methods

MethodsWelding · Brazing / soldering · Ultrasonic · Adhesives · Fasteners
Welding
Metals are fused with heat: MIG, TIG, stick, spot/resistance, laser, or friction stir welding (a spinning tool stirs the metals together without melting them).
Brazing / soldering
Parts are joined with a lower-melting filler metal, without melting the base metal (brazing above ~450 °C / 840 °F, soldering below).
Ultrasonic welding
High-frequency vibration bonds plastics or thin metals; common for plastic housings.
Adhesives & fasteners
Structural adhesives, rivets, screws, snap fits, and press fits. Which glue to use: Materials.
Machines
Welders and riveters: Machinery & Equipment.
ItemRemember
WeldingMelts the base metal
Brazing / solderingOnly the filler melts
UltrasonicVibration welds plastic
MechanicalRivets, screws, snap fits, press fits

Composites & Chemical Processes

Composite Layup & Chemical Etching

MethodsHand layup · Prepreg · RTM · Winding · Pultrusion · Etching
Composite layup
Carbon or glass fiber plus resin, combined by:
  • Hand layup: fabric wetted with resin in an open mold
  • Prepreg: fabric pre-impregnated with resin, cured under heat and pressure in an autoclave
  • RTM (Resin Transfer Molding): resin injected into dry fiber in a closed mold
  • Filament winding: fiber wound on a mandrel (pipes, tanks)
  • Pultrusion: fiber pulled through a resin bath and heated die (constant profiles)
Result: high strength-to-weight parts. The materials: Materials.
Chemical etching
Also photochemical machining: selective chemical removal makes thin, precise metal parts and PCBs, with no burrs or stress.
ItemRemember
CompositesFiber + resin: light and strong
PrepregPre-wetted fabric, autoclave cured
EtchingChemicals cut thin parts and PCBs

Finishing

Part Finishes

FinishesPolishing · Anodizing · Plating · Coatings · Steel · Heat
Mechanical
  • Tumbling (drum barrel or vibratory): many small parts at once; see the next box
  • Deburring: removes the sharp edges and burrs left by machining, cutting, and punching, by hand tool, file, abrasive, or tumbling
  • Polishing: finer and finer abrasives, then buffing compounds, to a smooth, mirror-like surface
  • Blasting: abrasive media for a clean, even matte texture (see the sand blaster on Machinery & Equipment)
Anodizing
Aluminum (also titanium): an electrochemical bath grows a hard oxide layer that is part of the metal, so it cannot peel. Type II: decorative, dyed colors; Type III (hard coat): wear resistant.
Plating
Electroplates a thin metal layer: zinc (cheap rust protection for steel), nickel (wear and corrosion; electroless nickel coats evenly), chrome (bright decorative, or hard chrome for wear).
Cerakote
Thin-film ceramic-polymer coating, sprayed and oven cured: tough, thin (~0.001 in), many colors; firearms, knives, high-temperature exhaust parts.
Powder coating
Dry powder sprayed electrostatically, then baked (~180 – 200 °C / 350 – 400 °F): thick, tough, many colors, no solvents; steel and aluminum.
Painting
Primer, then paint (liquid, spray); cheapest color and rust protection, less durable than powder coat.
Steel finishes
  • Passivation (stainless): an acid bath (nitric or citric) removes free iron and restores the chromium oxide layer; no change in look
  • Black oxide: chemical conversion to a black finish with no change in size; mild rust protection once oiled; tools, fasteners
  • Electropolishing: the reverse of plating; removes a thin surface layer for a bright, smooth, very clean, more corrosion-resistant stainless finish (food, medical)
Heat treatment
Changes the metal inside, not just the surface: hardening (heat and quench), tempering (less brittle), annealing (softer, easier to work), case hardening (hard skin, tough core).
ItemRemember
AnodizingAluminum only; grows from the metal (never peels)
PlatingZinc rust-proofs, nickel wears, chrome shines
Powder coatSprayed powder, then baked
CerakoteThin ceramic coating (guns, knives)
PassivationCleans stainless so it keeps resisting rust
Heat treatmentHarden, then temper; anneal to soften

Tumbling (Mass Finishing)

TopicsBarrel · Vibratory · Ceramic media · Plastic media · Shapes
What it does
Parts tumble with abrasive media, water, and a compound to deburr edges, round corners, and smooth or polish surfaces, many parts at once.
Barrel tumbler
A rotating drum: the load slides and cascades. Simple and cheap; slower (hours to days); good for large batches of small parts.
Vibratory tumbler
A vibrating bowl or tub: the load rolls in a spiral. Several times faster than a barrel, gentler on parts, and the parts can be checked during the run.
Ceramic media
More aggressive on both edges and surfaces; heavy and dense, so it cuts faster = less time. Best for hard metals: steel, stainless, titanium.
Plastic media
Less aggressive on surfaces but still deburrs edges, with more time. Lighter, leaves a smoother finish; best for soft metals (aluminum, brass, zinc) and before plating or anodizing.
Other media
Steel (burnishing to a bright shine); walnut shell or corn cob (drying and polishing).
Media shapes
  • Cones: reach into corners, holes, and recesses
  • Tetrahedrons (triangles, pyramids): sharp points for edges and inside corners
  • Others: cylinders, angle-cut cylinders, spheres, stars, ovals
Choosing size
Media must be clearly larger or smaller than any hole or slot, so it never gets stuck (lodging). Smaller media = finer finish and better reach; larger = faster cutting.
Loading
Roughly 2 – 3 parts media to 1 part parts by volume (more media for delicate parts), so parts do not hit each other; keep the compound and water topped up to keep the media cutting.

CeramicAggressive, fast: hard metals
