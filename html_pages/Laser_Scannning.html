3D scanning (also called laser scanning or reverse engineering) captures the real shape of a physical object as digital 3D data, which then becomes a CAD model, an inspection report, or a 3D-printable mesh. For the handheld scanner itself on the shop floor, see Machinery & Equipment.

How Laser Scanning Works

Principle & Technologies

MethodsLaser triangulation · Structured light · LiDAR · Photogrammetry · CMM arm
Basic idea
A scanner measures the distance from itself to thousands of points on a part's surface, many times per second; software stitches those measurements into a point cloud that represents the part's true shape.
Laser triangulation
A laser line or dot is projected onto the part; a camera next to the laser sees how the line bends over the surface. Knowing the fixed angle between laser and camera, geometry (triangulation) gives the distance to each point. This is the most common method in handheld industrial scanners.
Structured light
A projector flashes a series of striped or coded light patterns instead of a laser; one or two cameras read how the stripes distort over the part. Captures a wide area in one shot (a "flash"), so it is fast and very accurate for stationary parts, but needs the part and scanner to stay still during the flash.
LiDAR (time-of-flight)
Measures distance from the time a laser pulse takes to bounce back. Less accurate at short range than triangulation, but scans very large scenes or distances fast: buildings, sites, terrain (used for "Scan to BIM").
Photogrammetry
Builds 3D geometry from many overlapping ordinary photographs instead of a laser or projector; low cost and good for large or textured objects, but less precise than laser or structured-light scanning.
Articulated-arm / CMM scanning
A portable measuring arm (e.g., FARO) holds a laser-line probe on a jointed arm with encoders at each joint; combines point-probing accuracy with scanning speed, popular for inspection.
Accuracy vs. range
In general, the shorter the range, the higher the accuracy: close-range laser/structured-light scanners reach a few microns to a few hundredths of a millimeter, while long-range LiDAR trades accuracy (millimeters or more) for coverage over an entire building or site.

Scanning Workflow

StepsPrepare → Scan → Register → Mesh → CAD / inspect
1. Prepare the part
Clean the surface; shiny, dark, transparent, or black surfaces may need a thin, removable scanning spray (or matte powder) so the laser or light reflects evenly. Small positioning-target stickers placed on or around the part give the software fixed reference points.
2. Scan
Sweep the scanner around the part (handheld) or step it between fixed positions (structured light on a tripod) until every surface is covered from enough angles.
3. Register / align
Software stitches the individual passes or flashes into one aligned point cloud, using the target stickers or recognizable surface features as common reference points.
4. Mesh
The point cloud is converted into a watertight mesh (triangles, usually saved as STL or OBJ) that represents the part's outer skin.
5. CAD reconstruction or inspection
For reverse engineering, the mesh is rebuilt into an editable, parametric CAD model (STEP/IGES) in software such as Geomagic Design X. For inspection, the mesh is instead compared directly against the original CAD model to find deviations, with no rebuild needed.

Uses, Benefits & Limitations

TopicsApplications · Benefits · Limitations
Applications
  • Reverse engineering: recreating CAD for a part that has no drawings (legacy or discontinued components)
  • First-article and dimensional inspection: checking a produced part against its CAD model (GD&T, deviation color maps)
  • Digital twin / Scan to BIM: digitizing buildings, plants, or large assemblies for facility and renovation work
  • Fit and tolerance checks: scanning a part, 3D printing or machining a mating piece, and verifying fit quickly
  • Quality control: comparing production parts to a reference across a batch
Benefits
Non-contact and fast (captures complex, organic, or freeform shapes that are slow to measure by hand); produces data usable directly for CAD, 3D printing, or inspection reports; portable systems can scan parts too large or fixed in place to move.
Limitations
Struggles with reflective, transparent, or very dark surfaces without surface prep; cannot see hidden or deep internal features (undercuts, bores) the scanner has no line of sight to; point clouds and meshes carry no design intent (no fillets, holes, or features as parametric data) until rebuilt into CAD; equipment and skilled processing are relatively costly.
