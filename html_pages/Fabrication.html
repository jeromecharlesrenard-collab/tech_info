Overview

Subtractive vs. Formative vs. Additive

TopicsRemove · Shape · Add · How many parts
Subtractive
Removes material from a solid block: milling, turning, drilling, grinding, EDM, cutting. Tight tolerances and full material strength, but more waste and limits on internal shapes.
Formative
Shapes material without removing it: molding, casting, forging, sheet metal forming. Cheapest per part at volume, but molds and dies cost time and money.
Additive (AM)
Builds the part layer by layer from a 3D model (3D printing). Little waste, no tooling, complex shapes (lattices, internal channels); slower per part and usually weaker between layers.
CNC
Computer Numerical Control: the machine follows a G-code program. CNC mills and lathes (subtractive) and 3D printers (additive) are all numerically controlled.
How many parts
1 – 10: 3D print or CNC; 10 – 50: vacuum casting; thousands: injection molding, die casting, or stamping.
ItemRemember
SubtractiveCuts away from a block (machining)
FormativeMolds, casts, bends
AdditiveAdds layers (3D printing)
QuantityFew = print or machine; dozens = cast; thousands = mold

Subtractive (Material Removal)

Machining & Cutting Processes

MethodsMachining · Grinding · EDM · Laser · Waterjet · Plasma
Milling
A rotating multi-point cutter removes material from a stationary or moving workpiece: flat surfaces, pockets, slots, contours, and complex 3D shapes. Runs on 3-axis through 5-axis machines.
Turning (lathing)
The workpiece rotates while a stationary single-point tool removes material: shafts, bushings, threads, and tapers. Swiss-type lathes handle small, high-precision parts.
CNC machining
Computer-controlled milling, turning, routing, drilling, and grinding, driven by G-code from CAM software; repeatable, tight-tolerance parts from prototypes to mid volumes.
Drilling / boring / reaming
Making holes, enlarging them, and finishing them to a precise diameter and surface finish.
Grinding
An abrasive wheel removes small amounts of material for very tight tolerances and fine finishes. Types: surface, cylindrical, centerless, jig.
EDM
Electrical Discharge Machining: controlled sparks erode conductive material. Wire EDM cuts profiles; sinker EDM forms cavities. Suited to hardened steels and mold tooling.
Laser cutting
A focused laser beam melts or vaporizes sheet or plate: fast, precise, narrow kerf; metals, plastics, and wood.
Waterjet cutting
A high-pressure water stream, often with abrasive, cuts almost any material with no heat-affected zone.
Plasma cutting
An ionized gas arc cuts conductive metals; faster and cheaper than laser on thick plate, but less precise.
ItemRemember
MillingThe tool spins (flat faces, pockets)
TurningThe part spins (round parts)
GrindingTightest tolerances, finest finish
EDMSparks: hardened steel, molds
CuttingLaser = precise; plasma = thick and cheap; waterjet = no heat

3-Axis vs. 5-Axis Machining

Types3-axis · 4-axis · 3+2 · Simultaneous 5-axis
Axes
Three linear axes: X (left-right), Y (front-back), Z (up-down). Three rotary axes turn around them: A (around X), B (around Y), C (around Z). A 5-axis machine has X, Y, Z plus two rotary axes (often A + C or B + C).
3-axis
The tool moves in X, Y, Z and always points straight down. It reaches only what it can see from the top, so each new side needs the part flipped and re-clamped (a new setup). Simplest, cheapest, easiest to program: plates, brackets, pockets, slots, flat and 2.5D parts.
4-axis
Adds one rotary axis (usually A, a rotary table or "4th axis"): the part turns so holes and features can be cut around it, e.g. cams, shafts with flats, parts machined on four sides.
3+2 (positional 5-axis)
The two rotary axes tilt the part to a fixed angle and lock, then the machine cuts in 3-axis mode. It reaches five sides in one setup and angled holes and faces; the most common use of 5-axis machines.
Simultaneous 5-axis
All five axes move at the same time, so the tool stays at the best angle to a curved surface as it cuts. Needed for impellers, turbine blades, propellers, complex molds, medical implants, and some undercuts.
Why 5-axis
  • Fewer setups: one clamping instead of several, so better accuracy between features (no re-clamping error) and less handling
  • Shorter tools: tilting the part lets a short, stiff tool reach deep areas: less chatter, better finish, faster cuts
  • Complex shapes: smooth contoured surfaces and features that 3-axis cannot reach
Why not 5-axis
Much higher machine cost, more complex CAM programming and simulation, higher collision risk, often a smaller work area, and skilled programmers needed. For simple parts, 3-axis is faster and cheaper.
5-axis layouts
Trunnion (table-table: the table tilts and rotates; small to medium parts), head-head (the spindle head tilts and swivels; large, heavy parts), and table-head (one rotary axis in each).

Why Machining Is Much Faster Today

FactorsTool materials · Spindles · Toolpaths · Machines
Tool materials
Coated carbide, ceramics, and CBN (cubic boron nitride) cut steel several times faster than older high-speed steel (HSS) tools. In aluminum, cutting speeds can now pass 1,000 m/min (~3,300 ft/min).
Spindles
High-speed machining centers run at 20,000 to 40,000 rpm or more (standard ones ~8,000 – 15,000); older machines topped out around 6,000 to 10,000 rpm.
Toolpaths
Adaptive (high-efficiency) and trochoidal strategies keep the load on the tool steady, using the full flute length at a light side step, so the tool runs much faster without breaking.
Machine and control
Stiffer frames, faster drives, and controls that read the toolpath ahead (look-ahead) let the machine actually reach those speeds and hold accuracy.

Spindles20,000+ rpm vs. ~8,000 before

MachinesStiffer frames, look-ahead controls

Formative (Shaping Without Removal)

Molding, Casting & Metal Forming

MethodsMolding · Extrusion · Casting · Forging · Sheet metal
Injection molding
Molten thermoplastic is injected under pressure into a closed steel or aluminum mold. High tooling cost, but very low unit cost at high volume, with excellent repeatability. Full details: Injection Molding below.
Extrusion
Material is pushed through a die to make continuous constant-section profiles: tubes, rods, channels, frames. Aluminum, plastics, rubber.
Blow molding
An air-inflated parison (hollow tube of hot plastic) is shaped against a mold: hollow parts like bottles and tanks. Variants: extrusion, injection, and stretch blow molding.
Rotational molding
Plastic powder in a rotating heated mold coats the walls: large, seamless hollow parts with low tooling cost.
Thermoforming / vacuum forming
Heated plastic sheet is drawn over a mold by vacuum or pressure: trays, enclosures, packaging.
Compression / transfer molding
Material is pressed into a heated mold cavity; common for thermosets, rubber, and composites.
Casting
Molten metal is poured or injected into a mold.
  • Sand casting: cheap molds, large parts
  • Die casting: high volume, aluminum and zinc
  • Investment casting (lost wax): fine detail
  • Permanent mold casting: reusable metal molds
Forging
Metal is shaped by compressive force, hot or cold, which aligns the grain for superior strength. Open-die, closed-die, and roll forging.
Stamping / press forming
Sheet metal is cut and formed in dies (blanking, punching, bending, drawing, coining); progressive dies allow very high volume.
Deep drawing
Sheet is pulled into a die cavity: seamless cups, cans, housings.
Sheet metal bending
Press brakes bend sheet along straight lines: brackets, chassis, enclosures.
Roll forming
Strip passes through successive roller stations: long, uniform profiles.
Metal spinning
A rotating disc is formed over a mandrel: axisymmetric parts like cones and domes.
Powder metallurgy / MIM
Metal powder is pressed, or injection molded with a binder (MIM, Metal Injection Molding), then sintered: near-net-shape small, complex metal parts.

CastingPour metal: sand, die, investment (lost wax)

Vacuum Casting

TopicsMaster · Silicone mold · PU resin · Short runs
How it works
A master part (often an SLA print) is cast into a silicone mold; liquid polyurethane (PU) resin is then poured into the mold under vacuum, so there are no air bubbles.
Run size
About 15 – 25 parts per mold; ideal for 10 – 50 parts.
Strengths
Production-like finish and colors; resins that mimic ABS, PP, PC, or rubber (wide Shore hardness range); no costly steel mold.
Limits
Molds wear out; slower per part than injection molding; parts are PU, not the real thermoplastic.
ItemRemember
MoldSilicone, made from a printed master
MaterialPU resin poured under vacuum (no bubbles)
Run~20 parts per mold

Injection Molding

Process & Cycle

StepsClamp · Inject · Pack · Cool · Open · Eject
Principle
Plastic pellets are melted in a heated barrel by a rotating screw, injected at high pressure into a closed, cooled mold, held under pressure while they shrink, then cooled and ejected as a finished part.
The cycle
  • Clamp: the press closes and locks the mold
  • Inject (fill): the screw pushes forward like a plunger and fills ~95 – 98% of the cavity, controlled by speed
  • Pack / hold: switches to pressure (V/P transfer) to pack in more plastic as it shrinks, until the gate freezes
  • Cool: the part solidifies; meanwhile the screw turns and backs up to melt the next shot (recovery)
  • Open and eject: the mold opens and ejector pins push the part out (or a robot picks it)
Cycle time
Typically ~10 – 60 s; cooling is the longest step (often more than half), and it grows with the square of wall thickness: twice the wall ≈ four times the cooling.
Key terms
Shot (plastic injected per cycle); cushion (small melt left in front of the screw, ~5 – 10 mm, proof that packing worked); regrind (ground runners and rejects mixed back in, often up to ~10 – 25%).
Scientific molding
Also decoupled molding: fill by speed, pack by pressure, and set each step from measured data (viscosity curve, gate-freeze study) so the process is stable from shot to shot.
ItemRemember
CycleClamp, inject, pack, cool, eject
Fill vs. packFill by speed, pack by pressure
Cycle time10 – 60 s, mostly cooling
Thick walls2× wall = 4× cooling time

The Press

TopicsInjection unit · Clamp · Types · Sizing · Auxiliaries
Injection unit
Hopper → heated barrel (several heater zones) → reciprocating screw (melts and injects; a check ring stops melt flowing back) → nozzle sealed against the mold's sprue bushing.
Clamping unit
A fixed and a moving platen guided by four tie bars (or tie-bar-less frames for easy mold loading). The clamp is closed by a toggle (fast, common) or a direct hydraulic cylinder.
Press types
  • Hydraulic: robust, high force, lower price; uses more energy
  • All-electric: servo motors; precise, repeatable, clean, quiet, much lower energy use; standard for medical and precision parts
  • Hybrid: electric screw with hydraulic clamp, or the reverse
  • Vertical: mold opens up and down; ideal for insert molding and overmolding
Sizing: clamp force
Clamp force must hold the mold shut against the melt: projected area of all cavities and runners × ~2 – 5 US tons per in² (about 2.5 for many resins; more for stiff-flowing ones like PC). Presses range from ~5 tons (micro) to over 5,000 tons (car bumpers).
Sizing: shot
The shot should use about 20 – 80% of the barrel capacity: too small and the melt sits too long and degrades; too big and it is not fully melted.
Mold fit
Check the tie-bar spacing (mold width), minimum and maximum mold height, opening stroke (room to eject the part), and the ejector pattern.
Pressures
Melt pressure is often ~10,000 – 20,000 psi (70 – 140 MPa) and up to ~30,000 psi; the hydraulic gauge reads lower, multiplied by the screw's intensification ratio (often ~10:1).
Auxiliaries
Resin dryer (desiccant), mold temperature controller (water or oil) or chiller, robot or sprue picker, conveyor, granulator for regrind, color dosing unit (masterbatch).
ItemRemember
Two halvesInjection unit melts; clamp unit holds the mold
Press sizeIn tons of clamp
TonnageProjected area × ~2.5 tons/in²
ShotUse 20 – 80% of the barrel
All-electricPrecise and saves energy

The Mold

TopicsHalves · Runners · Gates · Ejection · Actions · Cooling
Two halves
A side (cavity, fixed half, injection side, usually the show surface) and B side (core, moving half, ejector side); they meet at the parting line. A standard mold base (DME, Hasco) holds the cavity and core inserts.
Feed system
Sprue (from the nozzle) → runners (channels) → gates (small openings into each cavity).
  • Cold runner: runners freeze and are ejected with the parts (scrap or regrind); cheaper mold
  • Hot runner: a heated manifold keeps runners molten; no runner waste, faster cycle; more costly mold
Gate types
Edge (simplest, trimmed by hand), submarine / tunnel (sheared off automatically at ejection), pin point (three-plate molds), fan and film (wide, flat parts), direct sprue, hot tip and valve gate (hot runner). Gate position controls weld lines, sink, and warp.
Two-plate vs. three-plate
Two-plate: one parting line, simplest. Three-plate: a second opening separates the runner from the parts, so gates can sit anywhere on the part.
Ejection
Ejector pins (most common, leave small round marks), sleeves (around round cores), blades, stripper plates (push the whole edge: thin-walled parts), air poppets.
Undercuts
Features that block straight ejection need moving parts: slides (side actions driven by angled cam pins), lifters (inside undercuts), collapsible cores and unscrewing molds (internal threads). Each adds cost: design undercuts out when possible.
Cooling and venting
Cooling channels drilled near the cavity (with baffles and bubblers for deep cores); conformal cooling (3D-printed inserts) follows the part shape. Vents, shallow slots (~0.01 – 0.05 mm deep, by resin) on the parting line, let air out so it does not burn the plastic.
Cavities
Single-cavity, multi-cavity (2, 4, 8, ... identical parts per shot, with a balanced runner), or family mold (different parts of one product in one shot).

HalvesA = cavity (show side); B = core (ejector side)
Flow pathSprue → runner → gate → cavity

Mold Steels, Classes & Finishes

TopicsP20 · H13 · 420 SS · Aluminum · SPI classes · SPI finish
Mold materials
  • Aluminum (7075, QC-10): prototype and bridge tools; fast to machine and cool, but wears sooner (~thousands to tens of thousands of shots)
  • P20: pre-hardened (~30 HRC); the general production mold steel
  • H13: hardened (~48 – 52 HRC); high volume, abrasive glass-filled resins, hot runners
  • S7: shock resistant (~54 – 56 HRC); shut-offs and slides
  • 420 stainless: corrosion resistant, polishes to a mirror; medical, clear lenses, PVC
  • Beryllium copper inserts: pull heat out of hot spots fast
SPI mold classes
Expected life of the mold: Class 101 (1 million+ cycles, hardened steel), 102 (up to 1 million), 103 (up to 500,000), 104 (up to 100,000), 105 (prototype, up to ~500).
SPI surface finishes
A (diamond polish: A-1 mirror for lenses), B (paper polish), C (stone), D (dry blast: matte). Textures (e.g. Mold-Tech) hide flow marks and scratches but need more draft.

Resins & Process Settings

TopicsDrying · Melt temp · Mold temp · Shrinkage
Drying
Hygroscopic resins absorb moisture and must be dried in a desiccant dryer before molding (PA, PC, PET, PBT, ABS); wet resin causes splay (silver streaks) and brittle parts. PP and PE need no drying.
Main settings
Melt temperature (barrel zones), mold temperature, injection speed, V/P transfer point, pack/hold pressure and time, cooling time, back pressure and screw rpm (melt quality).
Shrinkage
The mold is cut larger than the part. Amorphous resins (ABS, PC, PS, PMMA) shrink little (~0.4 – 0.8%) and evenly; semi-crystalline resins (PP, PE, POM, PA) shrink more (~1 – 3%) and warp more easily.

Part Design Rules

RulesWalls · Draft · Ribs · Radii · Bosses · Undercuts
Uniform walls
Keep walls even, typically ~1 – 4 mm (0.04 – 0.16 in); thick spots cause sink, voids, warp, and long cycles. Core out thick areas.
Draft
Taper walls in the pull direction so the part releases: ~1 – 2° per side (0.5° minimum); add ~1° per 0.001 in (0.025 mm) of texture depth.
Ribs
Stiffen with ribs instead of thick walls: rib thickness ~50 – 60% of the wall (prevents sink on the show side), height up to ~3× the wall.
Radii
No sharp corners: inside radius ≥ 0.5 × wall, outside radius = inside + wall; better flow, less stress cracking.
Bosses
For screws and inserts: wall ~60% of the nominal wall, tied to nearby walls with ribs; core them out.
Undercuts and holes
Avoid undercuts (they need slides or lifters); use pass-through cores or snap fits designed to flex out. Plan the gate, parting line, and ejector marks away from show surfaces.
Check
Run a mold-flow simulation and a design-for-manufacturing (DFM) review with the molder before cutting steel.
ItemRemember
WallsEven, 1 – 4 mm
Draft1 – 2° per side (more for texture)
Ribs≤ 60% of the wall (or they sink)
CornersAlways rounded
UndercutsAvoid (each one adds cost)

Costs, Lead Times & Variants

TopicsTooling cost · Lead time · Overmolding · 2K · LSR
Tooling cost
Very rough: aluminum prototype mold ~$2,000 – $10,000+; steel production mold ~$10,000 – $100,000+; multi-cavity hot-runner molds well beyond. Driven by part size, cavities, undercuts (slides, lifters), finish, and tolerances.
Lead time
Roughly 2 – 6 weeks for aluminum, 8 – 16+ weeks for hardened steel, plus first samples (T1) and tuning.
Part cost
Resin + machine time (rate × cycle ÷ cavities) + labor and finishing; often cents to a few dollars per part. Molding usually beats machining or printing from about 1,000 – 10,000 parts.
Variants
  • Insert molding: plastic molded around metal inserts (threaded inserts, pins, contacts)
  • Overmolding: a second material over a molded part (soft TPE grip on a rigid handle)
  • Two-shot (2K): two materials in one machine and one cycle
  • Gas assist: gas hollows out thick sections (handles) to stop sink
  • LSR molding: liquid silicone rubber, injected cold into a hot mold (seals, medical, baby products)
  • Micro-molding: tiny precision parts (medical, electronics)
ItemRemember
Mold costAluminum ~$5k; steel $10k – $100k+
Lead timeWeeks (aluminum) to months (steel)
Break-evenFrom ~1,000 – 10,000 parts
OvermoldingSoft grip on a hard part

Additive (3D Printing)

3D Printing Processes

ProcessesFDM · SLA / DLP · SLS / MJF · DMLS / SLM · Binder jet · DED
FDM / FFF
Fused Deposition Modeling (Fused Filament Fabrication): a nozzle melts plastic filament and lays it down line by line. Cheapest and most common (desktop printers); PLA, PETG, ABS, ASA, TPU, nylon, PC. Visible layer lines; weakest across layers (Z direction).
SLA / DLP
Stereolithography: a UV laser (or a projector or screen in DLP and MSLA printers) cures liquid photopolymer resin. Finest detail and smoothest surface (miniatures, dental, jewelry molds); parts need washing (IPA) and UV post-curing; standard resins are brittle.
SLS
Selective Laser Sintering: a laser fuses nylon powder (PA12, PA11). The loose powder supports the part, so no support structures; strong functional parts with a slightly grainy surface.
MJF
Multi Jet Fusion (HP): inkjet heads print a fusing agent on nylon powder, then infrared heat fuses each layer. Like SLS but faster, with more even strength; parts come out gray, usually dyed black.
DMLS / SLM
Metal 3D printing (Direct Metal Laser Sintering / Selective Laser Melting): a laser fuses metal powder into dense, complex metal parts for aerospace, medical, and tooling.
Binder jetting
A binder bonds powder layers, then the part is sintered: faster and cheaper for metal or sand (casting molds) at volume.
DED
Directed Energy Deposition: wire or powder is melted as it is deposited; large parts and repair.

Joining & Assembly

Joining Methods

MethodsWelding · Brazing / soldering · Ultrasonic · Adhesives · Fasteners
Welding
Metals are fused with heat: MIG, TIG, stick, spot/resistance, laser, or friction stir welding (a spinning tool stirs the metals together without melting them).
Brazing / soldering
Parts are joined with a lower-melting filler metal, without melting the base metal (brazing above ~450 °C / 840 °F, soldering below).
Ultrasonic welding
High-frequency vibration bonds plastics or thin metals; common for plastic housings.
Adhesives & fasteners
Structural adhesives, rivets, screws, snap fits, and press fits. Which glue to use: Materials.
Machines
Welders and riveters: Machinery & Equipment.

Composites & Chemical Processes

Composite Layup & Chemical Etching

MethodsHand layup · Prepreg · RTM · Winding · Pultrusion · Etching
Composite layup
Carbon or glass fiber plus resin, combined by:
  • Hand layup: fabric wetted with resin in an open mold
  • Prepreg: fabric pre-impregnated with resin, cured under heat and pressure in an autoclave
  • RTM (Resin Transfer Molding): resin injected into dry fiber in a closed mold
  • Filament winding: fiber wound on a mandrel (pipes, tanks)
  • Pultrusion: fiber pulled through a resin bath and heated die (constant profiles)
Result: high strength-to-weight parts. The materials: Materials.
Chemical etching
Also photochemical machining: selective chemical removal makes thin, precise metal parts and PCBs, with no burrs or stress.

CompositesFiber + resin: light and strong
PrepregPre-wetted fabric, autoclave cured
EtchingChemicals cut thin parts and PCBs

Finishing

Part Finishes

FinishesPolishing · Anodizing · Plating · Coatings · Steel · Heat
Mechanical
  • Tumbling (drum barrel or vibratory): many small parts at once; see the next box
  • Deburring: removes the sharp edges and burrs left by machining, cutting, and punching, by hand tool, file, abrasive, or tumbling
  • Polishing: finer and finer abrasives, then buffing compounds, to a smooth, mirror-like surface
  • Blasting: abrasive media for a clean, even matte texture (see the sand blaster on Machinery & Equipment)
Anodizing
Aluminum (also titanium): an electrochemical bath grows a hard oxide layer that is part of the metal, so it cannot peel. Type II: decorative, dyed colors; Type III (hard coat): wear resistant.
Plating
Electroplates a thin metal layer: zinc (cheap rust protection for steel), nickel (wear and corrosion; electroless nickel coats evenly), chrome (bright decorative, or hard chrome for wear).
Cerakote
Thin-film ceramic-polymer coating, sprayed and oven cured: tough, thin (~0.001 in), many colors; firearms, knives, high-temperature exhaust parts.
Powder coating
Dry powder sprayed electrostatically, then baked (~180 – 200 °C / 350 – 400 °F): thick, tough, many colors, no solvents; steel and aluminum.
Painting
Primer, then paint (liquid, spray); cheapest color and rust protection, less durable than powder coat.
Steel finishes
  • Passivation (stainless): an acid bath (nitric or citric) removes free iron and restores the chromium oxide layer; no change in look
  • Black oxide: chemical conversion to a black finish with no change in size; mild rust protection once oiled; tools, fasteners
  • Electropolishing: the reverse of plating; removes a thin surface layer for a bright, smooth, very clean, more corrosion-resistant stainless finish (food, medical)
Heat treatment
Changes the metal inside, not just the surface: hardening (heat and quench), tempering (less brittle), annealing (softer, easier to work), case hardening (hard skin, tough core).

Tumbling (Mass Finishing)

TopicsBarrel · Vibratory · Ceramic media · Plastic media · Shapes
What it does
Parts tumble with abrasive media, water, and a compound to deburr edges, round corners, and smooth or polish surfaces, many parts at once.
Barrel tumbler
A rotating drum: the load slides and cascades. Simple and cheap; slower (hours to days); good for large batches of small parts.
Vibratory tumbler
A vibrating bowl or tub: the load rolls in a spiral. Several times faster than a barrel, gentler on parts, and the parts can be checked during the run.
Ceramic media
More aggressive on both edges and surfaces; heavy and dense, so it cuts faster = less time. Best for hard metals: steel, stainless, titanium.
Plastic media
Less aggressive on surfaces but still deburrs edges, with more time. Lighter, leaves a smoother finish; best for soft metals (aluminum, brass, zinc) and before plating or anodizing.
Other media
Steel (burnishing to a bright shine); walnut shell or corn cob (drying and polishing).
Media shapes
  • Cones: reach into corners, holes, and recesses
  • Tetrahedrons (triangles, pyramids): sharp points for edges and inside corners
  • Others: cylinders, angle-cut cylinders, spheres, stars, ovals
Choosing size
Media must be clearly larger or smaller than any hole or slot, so it never gets stuck (lodging). Smaller media = finer finish and better reach; larger = faster cutting.
Loading
Roughly 2 – 3 parts media to 1 part parts by volume (more media for delicate parts), so parts do not hit each other; keep the compound and water topped up to keep the media cutting.
